How to backtest a trading strategy (without fooling yourself)

Backtesting means running your strategy’s rules over historical data to see how they would have behaved. Done well, it is the best evidence you can get before risking money. Done badly, it produces beautiful charts that mean nothing. This guide walks through a careful process step by step.

What is backtesting?

A backtest applies a fixed set of trading rules to past market data and records every trade the rules would have taken. The output is a history of hypothetical trades and summary statistics such as total return, drawdown, win rate and number of trades.

The key word is hypothetical. A backtest tells you how rules interacted with one particular stretch of history, under the assumptions you gave it. It is evidence to investigate, not a forecast.

How to backtest a trading strategy: step by step

  1. Write the rules down first. Entry, exit, sizing, markets and timeframe, decided before you look at results. If you can’t write a rule precisely, you can’t test it.
  2. Choose clean, appropriate data. Use data that matches what you’d actually trade, adjusted for splits and dividends where relevant, and long enough to include different market conditions.
  3. Hold some data back. Split history into a development period and a separate “out-of-sample” period you don’t look at until the end.
  4. Model real-world costs. Include spreads, commissions and slippage. Many strategies that trade often look good only because costs were ignored.
  5. Run the test on the development period. Record the results, including the bad parts: the worst drawdown, the longest losing streak.
  6. Check robustness. Nudge the parameters (a 20-day breakout becomes 18 or 22). If results collapse, the edge may be an accident of the exact settings.
  7. Test once on the held-back data. This is your most honest estimate. If it looks very different from development, take that seriously.
  8. Write down what you learned. Including what would make you stop trading it.

Costs, spreads and slippage

A historical price isn’t a guarantee you could have traded at that price. Every trade pays a spread and usually a commission, and fast markets add slippage: the gap between the price you expected and the price you got. A strategy making many small trades can see a large share of its theoretical edge disappear once realistic costs are included. Model costs conservatively; it is better to be pleasantly surprised than the reverse.

Overfitting: the most common backtesting mistake

Overfitting happens when a strategy is tuned so closely to past data that it captures noise instead of a genuine pattern. It usually creeps in gradually: you test, tweak a parameter, test again, add a filter, test again. Each change improves the backtest a little, until the rules describe the past perfectly and the future not at all.

Warning signs:

  • Many parameters or filters relative to the number of trades.
  • Results that depend on very specific settings.
  • A smooth equity curve with few trades.
  • Strong development results that fade sharply out of sample.

The defences are simple but require discipline: fewer rules, held-back data, robustness checks and a written record of every change you made.

Other biases to watch for

  • Look-ahead bias: using information that wouldn’t have been available at the time, for example today’s close to make a decision at today’s open.
  • Survivorship bias: testing only on companies that still exist today, which quietly removes the failures.
  • Small samples: 30 trades can look impressive by luck alone. More trades across different conditions give more reliable evidence.
  • Regime dependence: a strategy that only worked in one kind of market (say, a long bull run) may not survive a change.

How to read backtest results

Look past total return. Ask: how deep was the worst drawdown, and could you have lived through it? How many trades are there? How much of the result comes from a handful of outliers? What were costs as a share of profit? Did it behave similarly in different periods and markets? A modest, robust result is usually more useful than a spectacular, fragile one.

Frequently asked questions

Does a good backtest mean a strategy will make money?

No. A backtest shows how rules behaved on past data under your assumptions. Markets change, costs vary and overfitting can make noise look like an edge. Past performance is not a reliable indicator of future results.

How much data do I need to backtest a strategy?

Enough to produce a meaningful number of trades across different market conditions. There is no single rule, but a few dozen trades from one market phase is rarely enough to draw firm conclusions.

What is out-of-sample testing?

It means keeping part of your historical data untouched while you develop a strategy, then testing on it once at the end. It is one of the best checks against overfitting.

Can I backtest without coding?

Yes. Natural-language and visual tools let you define rules without writing code. You still need to understand the rules and the assumptions behind the test.
